Purpose

Turan Tech is an independent research lab founded by Ahmet Turan Taşdelen. Below, I tell it in my own words.

Vision

To build technologies that grow stronger without taking control away from people, where artificial intelligence and cybersecurity meet; to turn Turan Tech into a global technology brand born in Türkiye that makes complex systems simple, secure and understandable. To follow my dreams and be of use to the people around me, and above all to my country.

Mission

To turn curiosity into engineering, and engineering into trust. I do this with three principles:

  • Curiosity comes first. Every product starts with a question. I observe first, then build.
  • Simplicity in code, unpredictability in behaviour. The fewer the components, the smaller the attack surface. And I never leave the defence predictable.
  • Trust rests on verification, not on habit. To trust a system, you have to be able to audit it. AI is a powerful tool; the decision belongs to a human.

The belief that guides me

Mathematics is the language of everything, a structure that reason keeps building. My core belief is to unite curiosity with science, in Atatürk's words: “The truest guide in life is science.” No man-made system is flawless. So instead of claiming dogmatic perfection, I design layered, transparent, data-driven systems that anticipate their own flaws.

About me

Hello, I'm Ahmet Turan Taşdelen, a Computer Engineering student at the Faculty of Technology, Gazi University.

Personal profile

Who am I?

I describe myself as a bit of a wild spirit, and that wildness comes from curiosity: I have an endless curiosity about life, science and people. The trait I trust most is my power of observation: the first step to understanding something is to watch it carefully. Instead of memorising, I build the topic in my head and look for different paths when solving a problem.

Physics and mathematics are not just school subjects to me. I believe mathematics is the language of everything, and that it often tells us first what physics has not yet discovered. This curiosity led me to computers and artificial intelligence, and from there to system architectures.

My aim is to use modern technologies and their applications to create strong work that comes from this land and is rooted in curiosity and passion. My first big project, Şef, is the first step: an AI-assisted cybersecurity engine that aims for hardware isolation. While building it, I am trying out tomorrow's way of working today, with agentic AI systems and LLMs; and I write openly about my mistakes, because improving a system starts with seeing its flaws.

My goals are clear: to complete a minor in physics, to do a master's degree in AI in Europe and, in the long run, to work on secure AI systems by bringing hardware architecture and cybersecurity together.

Transparency note · 5 October 2026

I develop Şef in collaboration with Claude Code. I get help from Claude with writing code and drafting this log. The project's direction, the decisions, the interpretation of test results and the verification are mine.

Overview

Chief-Sef is a machine-learning-assisted software/hardware security engine that inspects PE features, Shannon entropy and API usage before a file is run. The current open-source prototype validates the static analysis layer in Phase 1.

Current progress

  • Random Forest model on real EMBER data, with consistent measurement on both sides: completed.
  • Phase 1 temporal test accuracy: 83.8%; same-period test accuracy: 90.1%.
  • False-alarm rate in independent validation on a real machine: 3.5%.

Expected performance and limits

These figures are experimental results from the prototype's README, not a production guarantee. The next goals are system-call monitoring with eBPF and NPU/TEE-based hardware isolation. The model's weakness against recent .NET threats is stated explicitly.

Feasibility report

My short assessment: This project is viable as a research and pre-screening layer, but it is not yet at the point of making a “safe” decision on its own. Seeing the measurement results in Phase 1 is a good start, but I still need to test separately whether the model behaves the same way across different time periods and new file types.

What concerns me most is how the data drifts over time, the risk of missing recent .NET threats, and how fast the Python prototype will run in real use. So I plan to run a small, measurable eBPF experiment on Linux first, and to take up hardware isolation once the threat model, the target device and the latency cost are clear.

My order: re-test the model with new data → measure eBPF events and resource usage → compare NPU/TEE options against a real use case. This approach keeps me from relying on a single accuracy score.
